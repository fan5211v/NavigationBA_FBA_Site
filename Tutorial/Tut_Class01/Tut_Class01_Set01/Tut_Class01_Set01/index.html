    Tutorial Class 01 Set 01 - Core Skills of a Business Scientist in the GenAI Era¶

    Learning Goals¶

    By the end of this workshop, you should be able to:

    • Define key concepts in the Uber surge-pricing case in clear, non-technical language.
    • Formulate a decision-relevant research question that is specific, measurable, and appropriately scoped.
    • Identify the data Uber would need to investigate suspected coordination and distinguish plausible signals from confounds.
    • Propose a first-pass detection idea and explain ways in which the analysis could go wrong.
    • Evaluate how different workflows with GenAI shape the quality and defensibility of your analysis and your ownership of the work.

    The Business Challenge¶

    Uber is facing a new challenge in cities like Sydney and Melbourne. Some drivers have found a way to game the surge pricing system by temporarily logging off in groups to reduce supply. This triggers a surge in prices—and when they log back in, they benefit from higher fares.

    While this behavior doesn’t technically break any rules, it raises serious concerns. Should Uber treat it as clever strategy or as manipulation? Should it intervene—and if so, how? Cracking down too hard could upset drivers and hurt morale. Doing nothing might damage rider trust and public perception.

    Uber’s analytics and operations teams need to investigate what’s really going on, what’s driving this behavior, and whether (and how) to respond in a way that balances fairness, incentives, and platform health.

    Prepare these Exercises before Class¶

    Prepare these exercises before coming to class. Plan to spend 30 minutes on these exercises.

    Exercise 1: Identifying the key concepts¶

    Watch this short news story: “Uber drivers creating artificial surge prices” (ABC News, 29 July 2025).

    In your own words, define the following key terms or ideas mentioned in the article:

    • Surge pricing
    • Algorithmic pricing
    • Platform manipulation
    • Driver incentives

    Tip: You don’t need to provide formal or technical definitions. Focus on explaining each concept in your own words, based on how it was used in the video.

    Solution

    These are example solutions, there are many ways one could answer these questions:

    • Surge Pricing: A dynamic pricing system used by Uber to increase fares when demand from riders exceeds the number of available drivers in an area. It’s designed to encourage more drivers to come online during peak times by offering higher earnings.

    • Algorithmic Pricing: The automated process by which Uber’s system adjusts fares based on real-time data like supply, demand, traffic, and location. Prices are determined not by a person but by algorithms using live inputs.

    • Platform Manipulation: When users—drivers in this case—intentionally alter their behavior to exploit the rules or algorithms of a digital platform to their advantage. In the video, this involves drivers coordinating to log off in order to trigger artificial price surges.

    • Driver Incentives: The rewards or earning opportunities Uber offers to encourage certain behaviors—like staying online during busy times, completing a certain number of rides, or accepting trips in high-demand areas. These incentives shape driver decisions and are central to how the platform functions.

    Exercise 2: Defining the Problem¶

    Imagine you’re part of Uber’s analytics + operations team investigating this in Sydney and Melbourne.

    Part A (3–4 sentences): Define the problem for Uber. Your answer must include:

    • What’s happening: one sentence describing the suspected behavior in concrete terms.
    • Why it matters: one sentence naming who is affected and what Uber risks (e.g., revenue, trust, retention, regulation).
    • What Uber needs to decide: one sentence stating the decision Uber faces next week (e.g., monitor, redesign surge, enforce rules).
    • How we’d judge success: one sentence naming one measurable indicator that would improve if Uber responds well (e.g., fewer extreme surges, shorter wait times, fewer cancellations, improved rider ratings, stable driver online time).

    Part B (one sentence): Write one research question to guide analysis. Your question should include:

    • an outcome (what you will measure),
    • a behavior/exposure (what you think is driving it), and
    • a scope (a location and/or time of day to focus on).

    Solution

    This is an example solution, there are many ways one could answer this question.

    Part A

    • What’s happening: In Sydney and Melbourne, groups of drivers may be coordinating short periods of logging off at the same time in the same areas, making supply look artificially low and triggering surge pricing before they log back on to earn higher fares.

    • Why it matters: This can raise prices and wait times for riders in a way that feels unfair, damage trust in the platform, and create reputational or regulatory risk—while also affecting Uber’s relationship with drivers if the company responds too aggressively.

    • What Uber needs to decide: Uber needs to decide whether to treat this as normal strategic behaviour to monitor, or as harmful manipulation that requires an intervention (e.g., changes to surge design, warnings, or enforcement).

    • How we’d judge success: A good response would reduce “spiky” or unusual surge events that appear to be driven by sudden supply drops, while keeping rider wait times stable and maintaining normal driver participation (e.g., driver online time doesn’t fall).

    Part B

    In Sydney and Melbourne during peak periods (e.g., weekend nights), do sudden clustered driver log-offs within a local area increase the frequency or intensity of surge pricing?

    Exercise 3: Proposing a way forward¶

    Your manager turns to you and says: "Where should we start?"

    Part A: Write 5 bullet points describing your approach. You must include:

    • one bullet on what context/institutional detail you’d want to understand,
    • one bullet on what data you’d want to find or request,
    • one bullet on what analysis or method you’d try first,
    • one bullet on what could go wrong / what might mislead you,
    • one bullet on how you’d communicate results to a decision-maker.

    Part B: Which TWO of the 8 Business Scientist skills will matter most here, and why?

    Solution

    Example solution – there’s many ways to approach this

    Part A

    • Institutional detail: Clarify how surge pricing works in practice in Sydney/Melbourne (e.g., how “surge zones” are defined, how often prices update, and what driver actions affect the algorithm).

    • Data to request: Pull time-stamped logs on driver online/offline status by location, surge multipliers by zone, trip requests, wait times/ETAs, cancellations, and basic context (weather, major events).

    • First analysis: Look for “coordination events” — unusually sharp drops in active drivers within a small area over a short time window — and check whether these are followed by spikes in surge pricing compared with similar times/areas.

    • What could go wrong: Demand spikes (events, rain), app outages, traffic disruptions, or normal shift changes could create similar patterns, so we could falsely label normal market changes as manipulation.

    • How to communicate: Summarize findings in a short manager memo with one simple chart (surge vs driver supply over time), a clear estimate of how often these patterns occur, and a “confidence/limits” section explaining what we can and cannot conclude yet.

    Part B

    • Data Discovery: Without the right logs (especially precise online/offline timing and location-level surge history), we can’t distinguish coordination from normal fluctuations.

    • Analytical Theory: This situation is full of ways to get fooled (confounds like weather/events, measurement limits, false positives), so we need to be explicit about what could go wrong and how we’d check robustness before recommending action.

    In-Class Exercises¶

    You will discuss these exercises in class with your peers in small groups and with your tutor. These exercises build from the exercises you have prepared above, you will get the most value from the class if you have completed those above before coming to class.

    In the workshop you will work in groups of 3–4 on the same business problem (Uber surge pricing in Sydney and Melbourne). Your group will work through the in-class exercises.

    To help us reflect on how different tools shape analytical thinking, each group will be assigned a different workflow. The goal is to notice how your workflow affects the quality of your reasoning, your confidence, and which Business Scientist skills you use.

    Workflow allocation

    Your tutor will allocate one of the following workflows to your group:

    • Search-only: You can use web search and your pre-class notes, but you must not use GenAI tools.

    • AI-only : You must use a GenAI tool of your choice to answer the questions, but your group must not browse the web or consult external sources during the task. In addition, you should not add your own analysis or explanations. Your job is to act like an reporter of the AI’s output:

      • You may copy/paste and format the AI’s output, but you must not change the substance. (If something is wrong/unclear, fix it by asking the AI to revise, not by rewriting it yourself.)
      • You may ask the AI clarifying questions (e.g., “define this term,” “give an example,” “rewrite more clearly,” “list assumptions,” “suggest metrics,” “format as a memo”).
      • If you need a judgment call, ask the AI to make it and explain its reasons.
      • If you disagree with the AI, you must resolve it by asking the AI further questions, not by inserting your own reasoning.

    • Hybrid: You may use any combination of search, AI, and notes in any way you choose.

    At the end of the tutorial, the class will reflect on how different workflows shaped your answers.

    Exercise 4: Choosing the right question to answer¶

    Your group will choose one research question to guide the rest of your work in the session.

    • Step 1: Propose four candidate research questions.

    • Step 2: Score each question from 0–2 on each criterion below (0 = weak, 1 = okay, 2 = strong).

      • Useful: Would answering it help Uber decide what to do?
      • Answerable: Could you imagine what you would measure to answer it?
      • Specific: Is it narrow enough to be answered in a short investigation?

    • Step 3: Choose the highest-scoring question and rewrite it to be as clear as possible.

    Solution

    No solution provided. Depends on what the group comes up with.

    Exercise 5: What data do we need, and how could we be misled?¶

    Using the research question your group selected in Exercise 4, your manager asks: "What data would we need to answer your question?""

    As a group, complete the following.

    Part A: Must-have data List 5–7 data fields you would request or create to answer your question. For each item, add one sentence explaining what it would help you observe or measure.

    Part B: What patterns would you expect to see? List three patterns you’d expect in the data if your hypothesis is correct.

    Part C: What else could explain the same patterns? List two alternative explanations that could produce similar patterns,

    Solution

    There are multiple ways one could tackle this question. Here we provide one example.

    Suppose the research question is: In Sydney and Melbourne during peak periods (e.g., weekend nights), do clustered driver log-offs within a local area increase the frequency or intensity of surge pricing, relative to similar times/areas without clustered log-offs?

    Note that this question features the following structure:

    (Outcome = surge frequency/intensity; Exposure = clustered log-offs; Scope = Sydney/Melbourne + peak periods + local area.)

    Part A: Must-have data (5–7 fields) — with why each matters

    A strong list usually includes time + location + supply + demand + pricing + rider experience + context.

    • Driver online/offline status with timestamps (by driver) - Lets us detect sudden drops in supply and whether they are clustered in time.

    • Driver location / operating zone at time of log-off (geofence/zone ID) - Lets us see whether log-offs are concentrated in the same area rather than spread across the city.

    • Surge multiplier (or price adjustment) by zone over time - Measures the outcome directly: when and where surge appears, and how large it is.

    • Trip requests (demand) by zone over time - Helps separate a surge caused by real demand spikes from a surge driven by supply manipulation.

    • Rider wait times / ETA (or pickup times) by zone - Captures rider experience and whether any surge coincides with a real shortage in service.

    • Cancellations (rider and driver) by zone/time - A surge episode might lead to more cancellations; also helps indicate instability in matching.

    • Context controls: weather + major events + app outage logs - Key confounds: rain/events create real demand spikes; outages can mimic coordinated log-offs.

    Part B: Signals (3 patterns we’d expect if coordination is happening)

    • Clustered supply drop: Many drivers go offline within a short window (e.g., 5–10 minutes) in the same zone.
    • Surge follows supply drop: A surge multiplier increase occurs shortly after the clustered log-off (in that zone), even when demand is not unusually high.
    • Re-entry timing: Drivers return online after surge rises (or trip acceptance spikes right after surge), consistent with attempting to benefit from the higher fare.

    Part C: Confounds (2 alternative explanations that mimic the same pattern)

    • Real demand shock: A concert ends / rain begins → demand spikes; drivers appear “insufficient,” surge rises even without coordination.
    • Non-strategic supply shock: App outages, connectivity issues, traffic disruptions, or normal shift changes create a sudden supply drop and surge.

    Exercise 6: Next Steps¶

    Using your work from Exercise 5, answer these two questions:

    • What is the first pattern you would look for in the data to investigate your question?
    • What are two outcome indicators you would track to answer your research question? For each, add a short reason.

    Solution

    Carrying the same example RQ over from Q5: In Sydney and Melbourne during peak periods (e.g., weekend nights), do clustered driver log-offs within a local area increase the frequency or intensity of surge pricing, relative to similar times/areas without clustered log-offs?

    First pattern to look for in the data

    • A strong first pattern is a simple, concrete comparison that links the exposure to the outcome with timing and location.

    • Example answer: Look for short windows (e.g., 5–10 minutes) where many drivers go offline in the same zone, and check whether a surge spike occurs in that zone shortly afterward (e.g., within the next 10–15 minutes), compared to similar times/zones without a clustered log-off.

    Two outcome indicators to track (with reasons)

    • Two good indicators often combine pricing + service quality.

    • Example answers: Surge intensity or frequency (e.g., average surge multiplier, % of minutes/trips with surge in a zone): Directly measures the outcome in the research question and captures how big/common surge events are. Rider wait time / ETA (e.g., average pickup ETA, % rides with ETA above a threshold): Tells whether surge corresponds to a real shortage affecting riders, and helps distinguish “price spike only” from meaningful service disruption.

    Other acceptable alternatives (if justified):

    • Cancellation rate (rider/driver) — indicates instability and friction during suspected events
    • Completed trips per hour per zone — shows whether matching/throughput changes during suspected events
    • Driver online minutes — helps check whether interventions/conditions harm participation (more for later, but can be justified)

    Exercise 7: Workflow reflection¶

    One of our aims today was to notice how different workflows shape the quality of your work and how much of it you genuinely feel you own.

    Before discussing with other groups, reflect on the four lenses below:

    • Speed: How quickly did you get to your answer? What helped or slowed you down?
    • Confidence: How confident were you in what you wrote? What drove that confidence (or lack of it)?
    • Defensibility: If someone challenged your work, would you feel able to defend it verbally in a meeting?
    • Ownership: Did the final output feel like your group’s work?

    Solution

    Here’s some examples of what you might experience in the conversation. Ultimately there is no right answer:

    Sample reflection: Search-only

    • Speed: We were slower to get started because we had to read and agree on what mattered, but once we had a direction our answers became clearer.
    • Confidence: Medium–high. We felt better when we could point to something we read, but we weren’t sure we’d found the “right” sources.
    • Defensibility: Fairly defensible—we can explain our logic and show where claims came from, though we might miss some angles.
    • Ownership: High. The output feels like ours because we had to decide what to focus on and how to phrase it ourselves.

    Sample reflection: AI-only

    • Speed: Very fast—we got full answers quickly and the writing sounded professional.
    • Confidence: Initially high because the output was fluent, then lower once we noticed we couldn’t verify claims or justify details.
    • Defensibility: Weak. If challenged in a meeting, we’d mostly be repeating what the AI said rather than explaining why it’s true.
    • Ownership: Low. The output doesn’t feel like our work—it feels like we assembled text, and some parts don’t match what we actually understand.

    Sample reflection: Hybrid

    • Speed: Moderate—we moved quickly when we used AI for structure, but slowed down when we tried to check and reconcile different inputs.
    • Confidence: Medium. Using multiple sources helped, but sometimes the AI and search results pushed us in different directions.
    • Defensibility: Better than AI-only. We can defend our main points because we checked a couple of key claims and made our assumptions explicit, but some details are still shaky.
    • Ownership: Mixed. The overall logic feels like ours, but some phrasing and a few claims feel “borrowed,” especially where we leaned on AI-generated text.

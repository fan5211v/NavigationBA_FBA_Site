    Ferdeer Case Demo 1: Who Are Ferdeer Retail's Most Valuable Customers?¶

    Part A. Cleaning and Analysing Data¶

    1. Introduction¶

    Research Question: Which groups of customers generate most of Ferdeer Retail's revenue, and how do these groups differ in who they are and in how their visits to the site turn into purchases?

    Method: This is a descriptive analysis. We summarise three years of customer, purchase and browsing data to describe what happened: we segment buyers with a rule-based method (RFM) and a data-driven method (K-means clustering), profile the resulting segments with grouped summary statistics, and compare how often site visits from different traffic sources and devices end in a purchase. We do not claim that any characteristic causes customers to spend more — loyalty tiers and campaign exposure were not randomly assigned — and we do not build a model to predict future purchases.

    (3) Data Cleaning¶

    The data documentation warns that the files are not analysis-ready. We clean them table by table, and for each table we follow the same three steps: inspect the problem, decide what to do, and apply the decision. Our general rule is to set a value that cannot be right to missing rather than delete the whole customer, and to delete a row only when the row itself cannot be used.

    Step 1: Customer tables

    customer_profile.csv has 10,100 rows for 10,000 customers. We first check whether some customers appear more than once.

    # customer_profile.csv has 10,100 rows, but there are only 10,000 customers.
# Count each identifier and keep those that appear more than once
profile |>
  count(cust_id) |>
  filter(n > 1) |>
  nrow()

    100

    # Are the repeated rows exact copies? distinct() keeps one copy of each identical row
profile |>
  distinct() |>
  nrow()

    10000

    100 customers appear twice, and after distinct() exactly 10,000 rows remain — so the repeated rows are exact copies and can be dropped safely.

    Three problems appear. Each of the seven countries is recorded in both upper and lower case (e.g. US and us), which would split one country into two groups. 30 customers have an impossible age (as low as −5 or as high as 999), although the documentation says all customers are adults. 200 customers have no gender recorded.

    We standardise the country codes with case_when(), set impossible ages to missing, and label missing gender as "Not stated" instead of dropping these customers. We also rename customer_id to cust_id, the name used in every other file, before joining the two customer tables.

    demographics_clean <-
  demographics |>
  # Use the same identifier name as all other files, so that joins are straightforward
  rename(cust_id = customer_id) |>
  mutate(
    # Standardise country codes to upper case
    cntry = case_when(
      cntry %in% c("au", "AU") ~ "AU",
      cntry %in% c("br", "BR") ~ "BR",
      cntry %in% c("ca", "CA") ~ "CA",
      cntry %in% c("de", "DE") ~ "DE",
      cntry %in% c("in", "IN") ~ "IN",
      cntry %in% c("uk", "UK") ~ "UK",
      cntry %in% c("us", "US") ~ "US"
    ),
    # Ages below 18 or above 100 cannot be right: set them to missing rather than dropping the customer
    age = if_else(age >= 18 & age <= 100, age, NA),
    # Keep customers with missing gender, but label them explicitly
    gender = coalesce(gender, "Not stated")
  )

customers <-

    The checks reveal four issues:

    • Duplicates: there are 17,800 rows but only 17,650 transaction IDs, and all 150 extra rows are exact copies of another row.
    • Impossible quantities: 50 transactions have a quantity of 0 or less, although a purchase involves at least one unit.
    • Missing values: 1,087 transactions have neither revenue nor product. We cannot tell what was bought or for how much, so they cannot contribute to spending measures.
    • Refunds: 312 transactions are flagged as refunded. Their revenue is negative (or missing), while no non-refunded transaction has negative revenue, so the refund flag is reliable.

    We drop the duplicate, impossible and unvalued rows. Because refunded purchases do not represent value the customer kept, we exclude them from all customer value measures and keep only completed purchases in a separate table, purchases.

    transactions_clean <-
  transactions |>
  distinct() |>                          # 1. drop exact duplicate rows

    qty >= 1,                            # 2. drop impossible quantities (0 or negative)

  ) |>
  mutate(purchase_date = as_date(time_stamp)) |>
  # Attach product category and premium flag
  left_join(
    products_clean |> select(prod_id, category_name, is_premium),
    by = join_by(prod_id)
  )

# Completed (non-refunded) purchases are the basis of all customer value metrics
purchases <-
  transactions_clean |>
  filter(refunded == "No")

    The traffic source has the same case problem as country (Email and EMAIL). 4,027 events have no device recorded. Session duration contains 750 values of −1 and 783 values above one hour, up to 999,999 seconds (about 11.6 days), which contradicts the documentation's note that a session lasts minutes, not days.

    The last check is important for how we analyse the events: almost every visit (a customer and session pair) contains exactly one event. We therefore treat each event row as one visit, and its event type (view, click, add to cart, bounce or purchase) as the outcome of that visit.

    We merge the traffic source labels, label missing devices as "unknown", set impossible durations to missing, and create two variables: campaign_linked (remember that campaign_id = 0 means no campaign) and purchased, which equals 1 if the visit ended in a purchase.

    events_clean <-
  events |>
  mutate(
    # Merge upper-case duplicates of the same traffic source
    traffic_source = case_when(
      traffic_source %in% c("Direct", "DIRECT")           ~ "Direct",
      traffic_source %in% c("Email", "EMAIL")             ~ "Email",
      traffic_source %in% c("Organic", "ORGANIC")         ~ "Organic",
      traffic_source %in% c("Paid Search", "PAID SEARCH") ~ "Paid Search",
      traffic_source %in% c("Social", "SOCIAL")           ~ "Social"
    ),
    # Keep events with no recorded device, but label them explicitly
    device = coalesce(device, "unknown"),
    # Negative durations and durations above one hour cannot be right: set to missing
    session_duration_sec = if_else(
      session_duration_sec >= 0 & session_duration_sec <= 3600,
      session_duration_sec,
      NA
    ),
    # campaign_id = 0 means "no campaign", it is not a real campaign
    campaign_linked = if_else(campaign_id != 0, "Campaign", "No campaign"),
    # Outcome of the visit: did it end in a purchase?
    purchased = if_else(event_type == "purchase", 1, 0)
  )

events_clean |>

    (4) Data Processing¶

    We now build the customer-level variables used in the analysis. Following the RFM approach from class, we describe each buyer by three measures computed from completed purchases:

    • Recency: whole months between the customer's last purchase and the analysis date, 1 January 2024 (the day after the data end);
    • Frequency: the number of completed purchases;
    • Monetary: the total amount spent, in USD.
    # Analysis date: the day after the last transaction in the data
analysis_date <- as_date("2024-01-01")

customer_rfm <-
  purchases |>
  group_by(cust_id) |>
  summarise(
    last_purch_date = max(purchase_date),
    frequency       = n(),               # number of completed purchases
    monetary        = sum(revenue_usd)   # total spend in USD
  ) |>
  ungroup() |>
  mutate(
    # interval() measures the time between two dates; %/% months(1) converts it to whole months

    (2) Rule-Based Segmentation: RFM¶

    We first segment buyers with the RFM method from class. Each of the three measures is converted into quintile scores from 1 to 5 with ntile(), and the recency score is reversed so that 5 always means "better" (more recent, more frequent, higher spend). Before using the scores, we check what the frequency quintiles actually contain.

    rfm_data <-
  customer_rfm |>
  mutate(
    r_tile = ntile(recency, 5),
    f_tile = ntile(frequency, 5),
    m_tile = ntile(monetary, 5)
  ) |>
  mutate(
    # Reverse R so that R = 5 means the most recent buyers, in line with F and M
    r_tile = max(r_tile) - r_tile + 1
  )

# Check what each frequency tile actually contains
rfm_data |>
  group_by(f_tile) |>
  summarise(
    n_customers   = n(),
    min_frequency = min(frequency),
    max_frequency = max(frequency)
  ) |>
  ungroup()

    | f_tile <int> | n_customers <int> | min_frequency <int> | max_frequency <int> |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1526 | 1 |  1 |
| 2 | 1526 | 1 |  1 |
| 3 | 1525 | 1 |  2 |
| 4 | 1525 | 2 |  3 |
| 5 | 1525 | 3 | 11 |

    This check reveals a weakness of quintile scoring on this data. Most buyers made only one or two purchases, so frequency quintiles 1 and 2 contain only one-time buyers, and quintile 3 mixes one- and two-time buyers. ntile() must still create five equal-sized groups, so it splits identical customers into different scores. This is a concrete example of the arbitrary cut-offs of rule-based segmentation discussed in class.

    (3) Data-Driven Segmentation: K-Means¶

    K-means clustering lets the algorithm group buyers with similar recency, frequency and monetary values, instead of relying on cut-offs we choose. Because K-means is based on distances, we first standardise the three variables with a recipe(); otherwise monetary value, measured in hundreds of dollars, would dominate recency (months) and frequency (counts).

    # Standardise R, F and M so that monetary (hundreds of dollars) does not dominate
# recency (months) and frequency (counts) in the distance calculation
rfm_rec <-
  recipe(~ recency + frequency + monetary, data = rfm_segmented) |>
  step_normalize(all_numeric_predictors())

rfm_scaled <-
  rfm_rec |>
  prep() |>
  bake(new_data = NULL)

# Verify: every scaled variable should have mean 0 and standard deviation 1
rfm_scaled |>
  summarise(across(everything(), list(mean = mean, sd = sd))) |>
  round(3)

    | recency_mean <dbl> | recency_sd <dbl> | frequency_mean <dbl> | frequency_sd <dbl> | monetary_mean <dbl> | monetary_sd <dbl> |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

    All three scaled variables now have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

    K-means does not choose the number of clusters for us. We use the elbow method on all buyers and, as in class, the silhouette method on a random sample of 1,000 buyers to keep the computation fast.

    Part B. Analytics Team Memo¶

    1. Research Question¶

    • Our research question is: which groups of customers generate most of Ferdeer Retail's revenue, and how do these groups differ in who they are and in how their site visits turn into purchases?
    • This question matters because marketing resources are limited, and knowing which customers carry the business and which visits convert shows where retention and acquisition efforts are most likely to pay off.

    2. Data & Approach¶

    • We combined all eight Ferdeer Retail files for 2021–2023, covering 10,000 registered customers, 16,236 completed purchases and 199,627 site visits.
    • Cleaning removed exact duplicate rows (100 profile rows and 150 transactions), standardised inconsistent country and traffic-source labels, set impossible ages, prices and session durations to missing, and excluded 1,132 transactions with no revenue or an impossible quantity.
    • We described each buyer by the recency, frequency and monetary value of completed purchases at 1 January 2024, and treated each event row as one visit, because 99.99% of visits contain a single event.
    • We segmented buyers with the rule-based RFM scheme and with K-means clustering (k = 4) on standardised RFM values, then profiled the segments with grouped summaries.

    3. Preliminary Findings¶

    • Revenue is highly concentrated: the top 20% of buyers by spend generate 50% of revenue, while the bottom 20% generate only 3%.
    • K-means identifies two high-value segments, VIP Spenders and Regular Buyers, which together make up 35% of buyers but generate 66% of revenue (Exhibit 1).
    • VIP Spenders place about five orders, spend $780 each and buy more premium and electronics products, while Recent Light Buyers and Lapsed Buyers place only one or two orders and spend under $150.
    • Loyalty tier lines up with value, but age, gender, country and acquisition channel are almost identical across all segments, so demographics do not identify valuable customers.
    • 2,373 registered customers (24%) have never completed a purchase, and most of them are Bronze members.
    • Visits from campaign-linked sources (Email, Paid Search and Social) end in a purchase about twice as often as Organic and Direct visits, and desktop visits convert better than mobile visits within every source (Exhibit 2).

    4. Assumptions & Limitations¶

    • We assume the 1,132 excluded transactions (about 6% of unique transactions) are missing at random; if they belong mainly to some customers, the value of those customers is understated.
    • RFM quintiles rely on arbitrary cut-offs, and because most buyers made one or two purchases, ntile() splits identical customers into different frequency scores.
    • The K-means segments depend on the three variables we chose and on k = 4, and the silhouette method slightly preferred fewer clusters.
    • Campaigns target particular segments, so the higher purchase rate of campaign-linked visits does not show that campaigns cause purchases.

    5. Open Questions for Feedback¶

    • Display and Affiliate campaigns received $1.2 million (38% of 2021–2023 spend), yet no visit is recorded under these channels, so how are these visits tracked?
    • Should the VIP segment be defined by K-means, or by a simple spending rule (such as the top 20% of buyers) that managers can update each month?
    • Should refunded purchases reduce a customer's value, rather than simply being excluded from it?

    6. Next Steps¶

    • We would repeat the segmentation for each year to see whether customers move between segments, especially from Regular Buyers to Lapsed Buyers.
    • We would link visits to segments to compare the traffic sources and devices each segment uses.
    • We would analyse the checkout experiment to test whether the new checkout designs raise purchase rates, particularly on mobile.

    Executive Summary¶

    We examined which Ferdeer Retail customers generate most of the company's revenue and how their website visits turn into purchases, using three years of customer, sales and website data. Two customer segments, VIP Spenders and Regular Buyers, make up one in three buyers but bring in two of every three dollars of revenue. These customers stand out by how they buy and by their loyalty status, not by their age, gender or country. Visits that arrive through marketing campaigns end in a purchase about twice as often as other visits, and desktop visits convert better than mobile ones.

    Key Insights¶

    • Revenue is highly concentrated: the top fifth of buyers generates half of all revenue, while a quarter of registered customers have never bought.
    • VIP Spenders, 8% of buyers, place about five orders and spend $780 each, buy more premium and electronics products, and more than half hold Gold or Platinum status.
    • Email, Paid Search and Social visits end in a purchase 11–12% of the time, compared with 6% for Organic and Direct visits, and mobile visits, the majority of traffic, convert least often.

    Business Implications¶

    • Losing a small number of high-value customers would remove a large share of revenue, so keeping VIP Spenders and Regular Buyers is the first priority.
    • Targeting by demographics is unlikely to find more high-value customers, because purchase history and loyalty status are far better guides.
    • Most visits happen on mobile, where visits are least likely to end in a purchase, so the mobile experience is where the largest number of sales opportunities is lost.

    Recommended Actions¶

    • Launch a retention programme for VIP Spenders and Regular Buyers, selecting customers by purchase history and loyalty tier.
    • Run a first-purchase offer for the 2,373 registered customers who have never bought, and a win-back offer for Lapsed Buyers.
    • Review the mobile purchase journey, and confirm how Display and Affiliate visits are tracked before reallocating campaign budget.
